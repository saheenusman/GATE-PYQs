# 2020 CS Q42: Classifying L1 = {wxyx} and L2 = {xy : |x| = |y|, x ≠ y}

## Question

Consider the following languages.

$L_1 = \lbrace wxyx \mid w, x, y \in (0+1)^+ \rbrace$

$L_2 = \lbrace xy \mid x, y \in (a+b)^*,\ |x| = |y|,\ x \ne y \rbrace$

Which one of the following is TRUE?

- (A) $L_1$ is regular and $L_2$ is context-free.
- (B) $L_1$ is context-free but not regular and $L_2$ is context-free.
- (C) Neither $L_1$ nor $L_2$ is context-free.
- (D) $L_1$ is context-free but $L_2$ is not context-free.

<details class="success" markdown="1">
<summary>Answer</summary>

**A**

</details>

<details class="info" markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

**$L_1$ is regular.** The two copies of $x$ look like a "match" problem, but $x$ may be a single symbol.

*Claim:* a string $s$ of length $n$ is in $L_1$ iff $n \ge 4$ and its **last symbol $c$ also appears somewhere at positions $2, \dots, n-2$**.

- If $s = wxyx$, the last symbol of $s$ is the last symbol of the final $x$. The same symbol ends the first $x$, at position $|w|+|x|$. That position is at least 2 (as $w, x$ are non-empty) and at most $n-2$ (as $y$ and the final $x$ are non-empty).
- Conversely, if $c$ appears at position $p$ in that range, take $w = s_1 \dots s_{p-1}$, $x = c$, $y = s_{p+1} \dots s_{n-1}$ and the final $x = c$. All four parts are non-empty.

A finite automaton can check this: guess the position, remember one symbol, and compare it with the last symbol. So $L_1$ is **regular**. I confirmed the claim on all binary strings up to length 13, comparing the definition with the characterisation: no mismatches.

**$L_2$ is context-free.** A string $xy$ with $|x|=|y|=m$ is in $L_2$ iff some position $i$ has $x_i \ne y_i$, that is, positions $i$ and $i+m$ differ. Such a string can be cut as $u\,\alpha\,v\,\beta\,z$ with $\alpha \ne \beta$ and $|v| = |u| + |z|$ (the two mismatching symbols are exactly $m$ apart). A grammar for this:

$$S \to AB \mid BA,\quad A \to XAX \mid a,\quad B \to XBX \mid b,\quad X \to a \mid b.$$

$A$ generates odd-length strings with an $a$ at the centre, and $B$ the same with a $b$. In $AB$, the centre $a$ of the first part and the centre $b$ of the second part sit exactly $m$ positions apart, where $2m$ is the total length, so they are a mismatching pair. I checked this grammar against the definition on all strings over $\lbrace a, b \rbrace$ up to length 12: the two sets are identical (5334 strings each).

**Why the others fail:**

- **(B)** says $L_1$ is not regular, but it is.
- **(C) and (D)** say $L_2$ is not context-free, but the grammar above generates it.

**Key idea:** "two equal copies" is a regular condition once one symbol is enough. "Some position differs" is context-free, because the mismatching pair can be generated from the centre outward.
