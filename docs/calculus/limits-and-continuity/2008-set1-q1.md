# 2008 CS Q1: Limit of a ratio as x tends to infinity

## Question

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x - \sin x}{x + \cos x} \quad \text{equals}$$

- (A) $1$
- (B) $-1$
- (C) $\infty$
- (D) $-\infty$

<details class="success" markdown="1">
<summary>Answer</summary>

**A**

</details>

<details class="info" markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

Divide the numerator and the denominator by $x$:

$$\frac{x - \sin x}{x + \cos x} = \frac{1 - \dfrac{\sin x}{x}}{1 + \dfrac{\cos x}{x}}.$$

Since $|\sin x| \le 1$ and $|\cos x| \le 1$, both $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ and $\dfrac{\cos x}{x}$ tend to $0$ as $x \to \infty$. So the limit is $\dfrac{1 - 0}{1 + 0} = 1$: (A).

- (B) $-1$ has the wrong sign, and (C) and (D) are wrong because the numerator and the denominator grow at the same rate $x$.

(L'Hôpital's rule does not help here: the derivative ratio $\dfrac{1 - \cos x}{1 - \sin x}$ has no limit.)

**Code check:** at $x = 10^3, 10^6, 10^9$ the value is $0.99861$, $0.9999994$, $0.99999999862$, approaching $1$.

**Key idea:** divide by the dominant term; bounded terms over $x$ vanish.

</details>
