# 2025 CS2 Q51: LL(1) and ambiguity of two if-then-else grammars

## Question

Consider two grammars $G_1$ and $G_2$ with the production rules given below:

$$G_1:\ S \to \texttt{if}\ E\ \texttt{then}\ S \mid \texttt{if}\ E\ \texttt{then}\ S\ \texttt{else}\ S \mid a, \qquad E \to b$$

$$G_2:\ S \to \texttt{if}\ E\ \texttt{then}\ S \mid M, \qquad M \to \texttt{if}\ E\ \texttt{then}\ M\ \texttt{else}\ S \mid c, \qquad E \to b$$

where $\texttt{if}$, $\texttt{then}$, $\texttt{else}$, $a$, $b$, $c$ are the terminals.

Which of the following option(s) is/are CORRECT?

- (A) $G_1$ is not LL(1) and $G_2$ is LL(1).
- (B) $G_1$ is LL(1) and $G_2$ is not LL(1).
- (C) $G_1$ and $G_2$ are not LL(1).
- (D) $G_1$ and $G_2$ are ambiguous.

<details class="success" markdown="1">
<summary>Answer</summary>

**C and D**

</details>

<details class="info" markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

**LL(1) test.** A grammar is LL(1) only if the alternatives of each non-terminal have disjoint First sets.

- $G_1$: the three alternatives of $S$ start with `if`, `if`, $a$. The first two **both start with `if`** (a common prefix), so they conflict: **not LL(1)**.
- $G_2$: $S \to \texttt{if}\ E\ \texttt{then}\ S \mid M$, and $M$ can also start with `if` ($M \to \texttt{if}\ E\ \texttt{then}\ M\ \texttt{else}\ S$). So $First(\texttt{if}\dots) \cap First(M) = \lbrace \texttt{if}\rbrace \ne \emptyset$: **not LL(1)**.

Hence (C) is true, and (A), (B) are false.

**Ambiguity.**

- $G_1$ is the classic *dangling else*. The string $\texttt{if}\ b\ \texttt{then if}\ b\ \texttt{then}\ a\ \texttt{else}\ a$ has two parse trees: the `else` can attach to the inner `if` or to the outer one.
- $G_2$ looks like the textbook unambiguous fix, but the `else` branch of $M$ is $S$ (not $M$), which lets an unmatched `if` appear in it. The string
 $\texttt{if}\ b\ \texttt{then if}\ b\ \texttt{then}\ c\ \texttt{else if}\ b\ \texttt{then}\ c\ \texttt{else}\ c$
 has two parse trees: the last `else` can pair with the innermost `if` or with the outermost `if`.

I confirmed both by counting parse trees with a dynamic-programming parser on all strings of up to 14 tokens: $G_1$ had 6 ambiguous strings and $G_2$ had 1 (the one above).

So (D) is true.

**Key idea:** a common `if` prefix breaks LL(1), and the dangling-else problem persists in $G_2$ because the else-branch can be an unmatched statement.

</details>
