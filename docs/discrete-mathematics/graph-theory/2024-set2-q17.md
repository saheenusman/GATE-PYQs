# 2024 CS2 Q17: Graphs whose adjacency matrix is its own inverse

## Question

Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a simple undirected graph $G$. Suppose $A$ is its own inverse. Which one of the following statements is always TRUE?

- (A) $G$ is a cycle
- (B) $G$ is a perfect matching
- (C) $G$ is a complete graph
- (D) There is no such graph $G$

<details class="success" markdown="1">
<summary>Answer</summary>

**B**

</details>

<details class="info" markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

$A$ is its own inverse means $A^2 = I$.

**Diagonal entries.** $(A^2)_{ii} = \sum_j A_{ij}A_{ji} = \sum_j A_{ij}$ (symmetric 0/1 matrix) $=$ degree of vertex $i$. Setting this equal to 1 gives: **every vertex has degree exactly 1.**

**Off-diagonal entries.** $(A^2)_{ij}$ counts common neighbours of $i$ and $j$. With all degrees 1, two different vertices cannot share a neighbour, so these entries are 0.

A graph in which every vertex has degree 1 is a **perfect matching**.

**Why the others fail:**

- **(A)** a cycle has every vertex of degree 2.
- **(C)** $K_n$ has degree $n - 1$. Only $K_2$ (also a perfect matching) satisfies degree 1.
- **(D)** such graphs exist, for example two disjoint edges.

(Brute force over all labelled graphs on 4 vertices found exactly the 3 perfect matchings, and none on 3 or 5 vertices.)

**Key idea:** the diagonal of $A^2$ lists the vertex degrees.

</details>
